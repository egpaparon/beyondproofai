Retaliation

It almost never looks like retaliation.

Nobody hands you a letter saying you are being punished for filing a claim. What happens is that the write-ups start, the schedule changes, and a supervisor develops concerns about work nobody mentioned in six years.

The law

Oklahoma prohibits it. Employers know that.

Start the record today

Dates, names, what was said, what changed. Keep it somewhere that is not a work computer, a work phone, or a work email account.

Oklahoma law prohibits an employer from discharging or otherwise discriminating against an employee for filing a workers' compensation claim in good faith. Employers and their counsel are aware of this, which is precisely why retaliation is rarely stated out loud.

What people actually describe when they call this office is a change in weather. Nothing was announced. It just became a different job.

What it looks like from the inside

  • Write-ups begin for things that were never an issue in six years.
  • The schedule changes to hours that are impossible with your restrictions or your childcare.
  • Overtime dries up, or the good routes go to someone else.
  • A transfer to a different site, department or shift, framed as operational need.
  • A performance improvement plan appears out of nowhere.
  • You are given work that violates your medical restrictions — and then written up when you cannot do it.
  • You are told the position was eliminated, and it is quietly filled two months later.
  • Nobody talks to you the way they used to.

Individually, each of these has an innocent explanation, and the employer will have one ready. That is the nature of these cases. They are built from timing and pattern, not from a confession.

What builds a case

Evidence you can gather without doing anything improper

You do not need to record conversations, take confidential documents, or confront anyone. Almost everything useful is either already yours or is simply a matter of writing things down.

The timeline

A dated list: when you were injured, when you reported it, when you filed, and then every negative change with its date. Timing is the backbone of a retaliation case.

Your own history

Past reviews, commendations, attendance records, raises. Evidence that you were a good employee right up until the moment you filed does a great deal of work.

Written communications

Emails and texts you already have access to — particularly anything about your restrictions, your schedule, or the discipline itself. Forward copies to a personal account.

Comparators

Who else did the same thing and was not disciplined? Selective enforcement of a rule everybody breaks is one of the strongest patterns available.

Witnesses

Names of coworkers who saw the change. You do not need to interview them — just record who was there. Do not pressure anyone.

Medical restrictions

Copies of every work restriction your doctor issued, and evidence of what you were actually assigned to do after it was issued.

Two mistakes

The things that damage these cases most

Quitting

It is completely understandable — the situation is intolerable and you want out. But resigning changes the legal picture considerably and hands the employer an easy answer. Talk to someone before you resign, not after.

Reacting

A confrontation, a walked-off shift, an angry message. Entirely human, and it will be the first exhibit in the employer's file. If you are being provoked, the most useful response is to document it and keep going.
